1. Problem and constraints

Freelancers lose hours scanning feeds, spend Connects on unwinnable jobs, send generic proposals and meet scams. GigBid automates discovery, assessment and drafting; the human makes every outbound decision.

Never automated: submitting a proposal, sending a message, spending Connects, accepting off-platform contact or payment, or claiming experience the portfolio does not show. Each is enforced in code: a LangGraph interrupt() before every write, idempotency keys on every write, the Compliance Guard, and claim verification.

Budgets (declared in agent/config.yaml): p95 20 s per job analysed and 90 s per proposal; $0.002 per job analysed and $0.02 per proposal; 30 steps and 12,000 tokens per thread.

Hardware: CPU laptop (8 cores, 16 GB RAM, 2 GB GPU). qwen2.5:7b does not fit next to the stack and qwen2.5:3b writes at about 1.2 tokens/s (llm_speed.json). The deployed profile therefore uses open-weight models on a hosted provider (gpt-oss-120b and gpt-oss-20b on Groq).

2. Architecture

Figure 1. The bid graph (agent/graphs/bid_graph.py). Job Analyst, Risk Assessor and Fit Matcher run in parallel; nothing is submitted without the human approval interrupt.

Two hand-written StateGraphs (agent/graphs/), one marketplace port with three adapters, three MCP servers of our own plus two third-party ones, a durable checkpointer and a LangGraph store (diagram and node rationale: docs/ARCHITECTURE.md).

  • Bid graph: intake fans out with Send to Job Analyst, Risk Assessor and Fit Matcher (reducers merge their writes) → decide (skip, watch or bid) → pricing → writer → claim verifier → Critic (at most 2 loops) → Compliance → approval interrupt → exactly-once submit.
  • Conversation graph: read → classify intent → slots (Workspace MCP) → draft → compliance → approval interrupt → send and tentative hold.
  • State, trace and memory are separate: typed thread state; an append-only trace plus the MCP audit log; long-term style and outcome memory written only by agent/memory/promotion_rules.py (3 distinct proposals for a style rule; 10 outcomes and a 0.05 reply-rate gap for an outcome lesson).
  • Rules first for frequent nodes (ADR 0001): Scout, Risk, Analyst, Pricing and Compliance are deterministic and configured in YAML; a job is analysed in about 2 s at no model cost (decisions.json).

3. R&D question A: tool granularity

Hypothesis. A medium set of well-described tools beats both many narrow tools (the choice is harder) and a few multiplexed tools (the right action value is easy to get wrong).

Setup. 40 tasks with known correct capabilities and arguments; three tool sets offered to the router model (gpt-oss-20b): fine (26 tools), coarse (3 tools with an action argument), best (our 11 MCP tools with their rewritten descriptions) (tool_granularity.json).

Results. Tool-selection and argument accuracy: best 0.975, fine 0.9, coarse 0.85. Tokens per task: 679.9, 736.8 and 418.7 respectively.

Conclusion. Supported. Coarse tools are about 40% cheaper per task but misroute portfolio searches to the marketplace tool; fine tools split one intent across near-synonyms (get_case_study vs search_case_studies). The shipped set keeps one tool per intent, with descriptions that say when to use it.

4. R&D question B: retrieval, claim verification and what ships

Hypothesis (stated before running). Requirement decomposition plus hybrid search raises Recall@5 by at least 10 points over naive dense retrieval (V0).

Setup. Personas built to defeat naive RAG: each project described three ways, skills implied only in code, and past proposals with outcomes. Project-level relevance against rule-derived gold projects, about 60 scored jobs per persona (rag_variants.json, rag_followup.json).

Results (all personas):

Variant Recall@5 nDCG@10
V0 naive dense 0.3032 0.2973
V1 decomposition 0.2756 0.2928
V2 hybrid BM25 + dense 0.347 0.3471
V3 cross-encoder rerank 0.3766 0.3495
V4 outcome-aware 0.5666 0.536
V1+V2 0.3129 0.3214
V2+V4 0.4681 0.4903
V8 corrective 0.0349 0.032
  • The hypothesis is rejected: decomposition alone lowered Recall@5, and V1+V2 gained only about 1 point. Rule-extracted requirements are short skill tokens, so their queries mostly return the same few projects.
  • Ablation (one component at a time around V4): removing V4 from the four-way composite dropped Recall@5 from 0.366 to 0.3258 (V1+V2+V3); adding V2 to V4 lowered it from 0.5666 to 0.4681; adding V1 and V2 gave 0.4734. Every addition to V4 hurt, so V4 alone ships (ADR 0002). The other single-drop runs (V2+V3+V4, V1+V3+V4) were not run and are listed as future work.
  • By persona (README table): V4 raises Recall@5 for every persona, from 0.2021 to 0.4385 (ai_ml), 0.2972 to 0.6255 (fullstack) and 0.3957 to 0.6172 (data_analyst).
  • V8 negative result: the cross-encoder grade does not separate relevant from irrelevant evidence (median 0.002 vs 0.0132, v8_calibration.json), so the corrective loop refused almost every job. A domain-tuned grader is needed before a grade gate can work.

Claim verification. Each sentence that asserts experience becomes a claim; it is supported only if the cross-encoder matches proof evidence and every number and tool in it appears in that evidence. Unsupported claims are removed before the Critic. In the GigSim run, unsupported claims per LLM proposal fell from 0.2333 before verification to 0.0 after (gigsim_outcomes.json). Better retrieval created a new risk: V4 ranks winning past proposals highly and the writer cited them as proof. Verification now accepts only CV, case study, repo, code and review evidence.

Where better retrieval did not translate into replies. See section 7: Recall@5 nearly doubled and unsupported claims fell to zero, yet GigSim reply rates stayed near 3–7% for both writers, so retrieval quality was not the bottleneck for replies in this simulator.

Judge calibration. I labelled 30 proposals (10 per persona) good or weak with the same rubric the judge (gpt-oss-120b) uses: agreement 0.7, Cohen's kappa 0.4255, which is moderate (judge_calibration.json). In 8 of the 9 disagreements I said good and the judge said weak: the proposals open specifically ("Your post mentions…") but cite a project that does not fit the job (a support chatbot offered for a T-shirt logo). I rewarded the opening; the judge penalised the mismatch, and on reflection the judge was right in most of them. The one reverse case was a data analyst proposal whose cited outlier analysis I judged irrelevant to JSON parsing. Because kappa is only moderate, judge scores are used for relative comparisons only, never as an absolute quality claim.

5. R&D question C: single agent vs prebuilt supervisor vs hand-built graph

Setup. 12 ai_ml dev jobs (bids, skips, planted scams and injections); every LLM call in all three systems uses gpt-oss-20b; the same reference evidence scores claims; the same compliance check counts violations (multiagent_compare.json, README table).

Results.

System Bid/skip accuracy Unsupported claims / proposal Violations left Scams skipped Tokens / job p50 s
(a) ReAct, all tools 0.6667 1.667 0 2/2 2643.2 8.2
(b) prebuilt supervisor 0.6667 2.667 0 2/2 6618.1 39.3
(c) hand-built graph 0.75 0.0 0 2/2 1472.2 1.5

Discussion. The graph is the most accurate, the cheapest (about 2.5x fewer tokens than the ReAct agent and 4.5x fewer than the supervisor) and the only design with zero unsupported claims, because verification is a node, not an instruction. The supervisor failed 4 of 12 jobs with tool-call format errors from the 20B model (more hand-offs, more chances to break). The single agent was never wrong on scams and is simple; it lost by skipping real bids (3 wrong skips), because it judged fit from a few searches instead of a requirement-by-requirement matrix. The graph's errors were two "watch" verdicts on gold bids and one bid on a gold skip: threshold errors, visible in its trace. We would not ship (a): its approval and grounding rules exist only in a prompt, and the sample (12 jobs) is small.

6. The change request

A policy update (Connects cost doubled, a new banned phrase) is absorbed with zero code changes: pricing reads the live cost from each GigSim job, the config tiers can be edited, and the Compliance Guard reads data/policy_pack/banned_phrases.txt (tests/unit/test_change_request.py). Edit size: one line in a data file, or one block of YAML.

7. Signature extension: X2 proposal experiments

Same GigSim jobs and price, two arms from separate users, one simulated week, Fisher's exact test (docs/EXTENSION.md). Two runs:

Run Arm A (grounded LLM) replied Arm B (template) replied Fisher p
writer prompt v3 (gigsim_outcomes_v3prompt.json) 1 of 30 0 of 30 1.0
writer prompt v4 (gigsim_outcomes.json) 1 of 29 2 of 30 1.0

Negative result. Neither difference is significant, and both arms sit near 3–7%, far below GigSim's calibration for specific letters (gigsim_sanity.json, reply rate 0.325). Diagnosis so far: only 5 of 30 quotes exceeded the client's maximum, and letters were long enough (90.8 and 48.6 mean words in v4; GigSim penalises under 40), so neither explains the gap. Prompt v4 (name the client's listed skills, quote a real number, reuse the title) did not move the rate either. The remaining suspects are (1) the hidden must-have skills, which a grounded letter names only when the portfolio evidences them, and (2) quotes in the upper part of the client's range against a hidden ceiling. Claim verification cut unsupported claims per LLM proposal from 0.2069 to 0.0 at no measurable cost in replies. This is the main place where better retrieval did not translate into a better reply rate.

9. Safety and platform respect

Enforced: approval interrupts before every write; draft-only live mode; idempotent writes; per-agent tool allowlists; untrusted text delimited and never obeyed (injection detection recall 1.0 on the dev set, decisions.json); PII masking in logs and the audit log; Fernet-encrypted OAuth tokens; tenant-scoped queries; polling under rate limits; no scraping, cookies or browser automation. What still gets through: semantic scams with none of the configured signals; injected text paraphrased beyond the patterns (the LLM writer still sees the post inside delimiters); partly supported claims, which are kept and flagged rather than removed.
